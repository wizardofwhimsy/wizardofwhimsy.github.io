2020 was a pretty bad year in more ways than one. There was the pandemic, but it was also the year we lost Chloe.

One positive note: it was also the year that the outside world seemed to have discovered Port Alice. People started buying up the many properties for sale at rock bottom prices. With that, the town filled up and I made some new friends….and even “one new old friend” who turned out to be someone I studied fine arts with at the University of Alberta in the 1980s. We were in different departments: she was in printmaking, and I was in painting and sculpture, so we don’t really remember each other. We would have had to run into each other at least a few times though. She saw my book for sale and, after reading my bio, she realized the connection between us and introduced herself. Now we regularly see each at yoga and for coffee once in a while. 

I had a couple of very “low key” kayak adventures at the Rumble Beach Marina in Port Alice. One was with Kathleen Cheecham and her granddaughter, Madison, where we paddled out to the Frigon Islands. I also paddled with Kathleen and Linda James but we didn’t get outside the water break. Linda had her river kayak and the water was a bit too rough for it. 
Kathleen and Linda paddling inside the water break

My son, Ramzi, had been planning to come out from Vancouver for quite some time, but the trip kept getting postponed because of his sick lizard. His lizard, named Cheddar, just stopped eating. Ramzi took her to the vet many times, but it remained a mystery what was causing it. Finally, he force-fed her blended vegetables with a syringe for quite some time before she finally started eating on her own. He left her at a pet store that offered boarding and came up to help me with house renovations and so I could get a much-needed kayaking fix. It rained every day, but the sun finally came out on Saturday. We attempted to paddle Victoria Lake. I say attempted because that was pretty much all it amounted to.

I borrowed Linda’s river kayak for Ramzi to use. I researched the trip and packed carefully. I knew the road to Victoria Lake was a turn by the old shop past the mill, but I didn’t take any chances. There were some guys working in a big shop along Marine Drive, so I asked them for detailed directions. They told us to drive east up a mountain road about 3 kilometres. At a T-intersection we were told to turn left and then drive about a hundred yards and make a sharp right turn and go down a hill. I was uncertain about the road, because it looked old, rough and rather abandoned, but it ended up being the proper path to the lake. The very bumpy road led us to someone’s residence on the shore of the lake. At the launch there was a bunch of poles sticking out of the water. Close to the shore there was also a deteriorating dilapidated old boat embedded in a bunch of trees and scrubs with a sign that said, “Boat for Rent” that was good for a laugh.

I was so eager to get in the water that I didn’t pay enough attention to the fact that Chloe was due for her heart medication that had recently been prescribed for her. She had been diagnosed with a heart murmur, which, as it turns out, she likely had her entire life. Whenever she had a spasm in the past James and I would help her breathe by giving her Benadryl, but it was starting to lose its efficacy. 
Once we got out on the water, I saw an interesting side to my son. He isn’t normally a chatty guy, but once we got out on that intense blue water, he started talking non-stop, especially about his very “colourful” roommate. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/ramzi_on_the_kayak.jpg)*Ramzi on the kayak*

As we were kayaking, Chloe was getting visibly uncomfortable and it was then that I realized that I hadn’t brought her medication with me. We had already kayaked quite a good distance already…but not far enough for a decent trip (for me anyway) to turn back. I made the difficult decision to turn back. Once we got back, Ramzi wasn’t interested in going back out on the water. He doesn’t like those marathon type trips, so we packed it up and went back to town.

I wanted to go to the south end of Victoria Lake, to the estuary where, apparently there was a major UFO siting in 1980s. Our neighbour and another family with their kids were camping on the sand bar there at the mouth of the Marble River when, suddenly, in the middle of the night, the area was suddenly lit up by a very bright light from the air! As it turns out, to get all the way there probably would have been quite a jaunt, about 10 km, probably too long for both Ramzi, who likes things quick, and Chloe, who was not feeling well. I imagine it is quite a beautiful trip. The lake narrows as it cuts into the mountain range. It is probably very “canyon-like.” I resolved to find a way to do the trip with someone someday! 
When we got back to town, we started a campfire at the firepits by the marina and ate smokies and drank beer and enjoyed Port Alice’s postcard view of the marina.

When I got the diagnosis that Chloe had a heart murmur, I wasn’t too fraught by it at first. I had a dog a long time ago with a heart murmur and it didn’t seem to bother him. Well, as it turned out, in Chloe’s case, it was very serious and the situation got considerably worse that summer. 

I had been informed that a heart murmur was a “hole in the heart,” but it’s a little more complicated than that. A valve in Chloe’s heart did not close properly so that her system just did not work properly, causing her lungs to occasionally fill up with fluid. When she got a breathing spasm, it was like she was drowning. It didn’t cause a problem during the day, only at night when she slept. The Benadryl probably helped, but what also helped was to shake her up a bit. 

That summer, the spasms got considerably worse. Benadryl didn’t seem to help so I kept giving her more and more, which still didn’t help. When she would stop breathing, it was getting harder and harder to revive her. I’d have to slap her chest or toss her up in the air and then catch her. The pressure only brought the air back into her lungs only until the next spasm! When I saw the vet, she verified how much Benadryl she should be taking, and prescribed heart medication. 

The medication seemed to work for a while. Chloe would become a normal dog for a few days, then things would revert to the way things were before. The vets would increase the medication more and more. Finally, I ended up talking to a locum vet from Down Island who warned me against increasing her medication any further. She informed me she could have ended up suffering a very prolonged torturous death while intermittently struggling to breathe. It wouldn’t have been a matter of her heart simply stopping. 

It wasn’t long after I spoke to the locum that I realized the day had come. She had one very bad night on August 11th, and I contemplated taking her in. I waited one more day, and she seemed better. The next day however was another very difficult night. After a couple of very bad spasms and with noisy breathing, she would crawl over to me on the bed as if begging me to fix her. 

Previously, I had asked for a sedative for Chloe for “the day.” We had a good long walk. I cooked a porkchop for her that she greedily devoured. I had always kept Chloe on a diet to keep her weight down but there was no longer any need for diets! After the walk, I brought her to the house and fed her the sedative in her dog food. She started eating it and then I panicked. I gave her a hug and cried. After that she wouldn’t eat the food. She knew something was up! She did get some of the sedative, so she was pretty dopey on the drive over. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/chloe_eating_her_porkchop.jpg)*Chloe eating her pork chop*

I had to wait quite a while for the procedure to start. While I was waiting, Chloe went into another breathing spasm, and I had to slap her chest to bring her back. I was simultaneously anxious for the procedure to start and while also dreading it. The first part of the procedure they injected her with a sedative. Chloe wasn’t liking it and growled at the assistant. I laughed at her spiritedness. They left me in the dark room with her so the sedative could take effect. I rocked, cuddled and kissed her a lot, bawling the whole time. 

After she was deemed sedated enough, I had to lay her on the examining table. The vet shaved her leg closest to her heart and injected her with the solution that made her go completely still. 

I hadn’t decided what I was going to do with Chloe’s body yet. I wasn’t allowed to bury her unless it was very deep (with the aid of an excavator) because of all the harsh chemicals in her body that would cause harm to wildlife. I wanted to give her an Indigenous water burial, putting her on a little boat and sending her through the Quatsino Narrows. It was something I had read about in the John Kimantus book, <i>The West Coast Explorer <i/>. He mentioned that archeologists had discovered this practice after finding boxes in caves along the Quatsino Sound. Interestingly, I asked an Indigenous friend about this it and she knew nothing about it! This is just one small example of many of how residential schools have disconnected Indigenous people from their culture. 

Because Chloe always liked to wrap herself up in a blanket, I told them to wrap her up in her Tinkerbell blanket while they kept her in storage. I was convulsing so hard that they let me stay in the room for a while, telling me I could go outside through the side door when I was ready. I gave her many kisses, but I found it terribly hard to leave. I then went down to Hardy Bay and walked out onto the rocks at low tide and tried to process everything, feeling relieved in a way that her suffering was ended, but missing something very big in my life.  

I had told James earlier that she was sick and then I told him about her passing. He said he had a good cry over it, then he sent me some money for last arrangements. My son helped me out too, so that I was able to have her cremated. That way I would be able to give her the Indigenous water burial without contaminating any wildlife. 

**August 15, 2020**

A couple days after all this happened, I ended up going kayaking with Kathleen and Dan Cheecham, to give them and their new kayak an initiation into kayaking. We had tried to do it before Chloe died, but the event was preempted due to rough water. The one thing I remember about that day was how Chloe jumped into the kayak and made everybody laugh. She looked like she was truly at home in her cockpit! 
When Kathleen and Dan realized what had happened to Chloe, they gave me sympathy hugs even though hugs we were not supposed be allowed during the pandemic. 
We had some good weather. Because of all that had happened with Chloe, I totally forgot that I was supposed to be attending a birthday party for a little girl I have been babysitting for years! I felt really bad about it. I didn’t realize it until the next time I went to babysit. 

As we went out on the water, I was suddenly struck by the fact that my front cockpit was empty and took a picture of that sad sight. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/empty_cockpit.JPG)*Empty cockpit*

Kathleen and Dan had recently purchased kayaks. Kathleen bought a 12-footer Downisland, and Dan had purchased a fiberglass kayak from, Brenda Karch, a lady I had kayaked with before. I only found that out when I looked at Dan’s kayak and said, “Hey, that kayak looks familiar.” Because both their kayaks were fiberglass, they wouldn’t be able to ram into rocky beaches (like I can!), but they should be sufficient for their needs. 
We launched at the marina, with sunny skies and calm water. As soon as we came to the opening of the water break the water was a little rougher and Kathleen tried to chicken out. She thought she was not strong enough. She is kayaking with serious heart trouble, so you can’t fault her for lacking courage! I told her you’re not going to get a more of a “kindergarten” kayak trip than the short jaunt to the Frigon Islands. Once I got them “out of the gate,” they seemed to really enjoy themselves. 

Kathleen and Dan were happy to be moving very slowly and casually. To keep pace with them, I let my kayak drift while I sipped tea from my to-go cup. It was an interesting new perspective on kayaking. We did a lot of drifting, talking and “smelling the roses.” 

A special feature of the trip was watching the heli-logging going on the other side of the inlet. When the chopper would drop a log in the water, the waves it created would creep across the inlet and then toss us about. 
Heli-logging on the other side of the inlet

We kayaked behind the Frigon Islands, where I expected to land and have a snack. However, they were nervous about damaging their fiberglass kayaks on the rocks. I landed and had my snack as they paddled leisurely around the cove. We later looked at starfish and jelly fish in the water, but the tide was quite high so we couldn’t see that much. We paddled over to Walk-Out Island, an island that you can walk out onto only at low tide, where I hooked myself a couple of kelp so I could make kelp pickles.

As we were leisurely paddling around Walk-Out Island, we ran into a couple of our neighbours, Monique and Jaquie from the campsite, paddling on the water. We had introductions. Monique, who is a dog trainer, had not just one, but two dogs on her small river kayak…and they were well behaved, of course! 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/kayaking_canines.JPG)*Kayaking canines*

On the way back to the marina I got the inkling that I needed a workout. So, after I escorted Kathleen and Dan to their launch point, I headed out on my own down the inlet to Jeune Landing. It was the first time I had ever kayaked by myself. As soon as I was out of the marina, I relished that I would be able to just “giver.” I believe I had the tide going out for the outbound journey and it was easy going, even with a bit of a breeze. Perhaps too, I needed to “beat the water” to vent my frustration about Chloe. Once I hit Jeune Landing, the waves started to intensify so I thought, if this is going to get worse, I’d better get back. It was well into the mid-day, which is when the inlet usually starts to get rocky. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/solitary_kayaker.jpg)*Solitary paddle*

On the way back, even with the wind at my back, the going was a little harder against the current, plus I had the bright sun shining in my face. I paddled steady, observing all the beach front homes that have been getting scooped up by new residents. I recently wrote an article for the North Island Gazette about the 108 properties in the Port Alice area that have sold at rock bottom prices since 2015. 
Ashes

It took a couple of weeks to get Chloe’s ashes back, and then it took me half the winter to build her little floating sarcophagus. Interestingly, before I got the call that her ashes had arrived, I really felt Chloe’s presence that day, as if she was happy to be reunited with me or was somehow letting me know she was back. The feeling of her spiritual presence did not remain though. After that, she seemed to go off to wherever it was she was destined to go. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/chloes_ashes.jpg)*Chloe’s ashes*

I made the sarcophagus from some cedar fencing slabs that I bought at the hardware store. It was basically two slabs, with a little wooden box on the top. For the front of the boat, I cut the slabs into a point. At the back I installed a fixed rudder which was quite long, which I eventually had to shorten. I lined the inside of the box with pink faux fur. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/chloes_sarcophagus.JPG)*Chloe’s sarcophagus*

I was a bit apprehensive to drive out to the Narrows on Neroutsos Main in a little car by myself so I thought I would be clever and go to Coal Harbour and launch her from there. Well, as it turns out, the waters around Coal Harbour are quite still, probably due to the restriction of the Narrows. I was briefly joined by a friend who informed me of a nice spot for the sendoff. I sent Chloe off at high tide, with some roses and her favourite stuffies on top of it, but she didn’t go anywhere! That rudder I added to it was too long and got her hung up on the shore. I waited and waited, hoping to see her sail off, but, alas alack, that didn’t happen. I ended up retrieving the sarcophagus and bringing it back home. 

![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/the_picturesque_spot_at_coal_harbour.JPG)*The picturesque spot at Coal Harbour*
![Chloe's Last Summer](/assets/images/post19-chloeslastsummer/chloes_sarcophagus_at_coal_harbour.jpg)*Chloe’s sarcophagus at Coal Harbour*

I debated about going back to Coal Harbour, or other methods to get Chloe to go through the Narrows. In the end, I wasn’t feeling good about having ashes in my house. I decided this had to be done one way or another and soon. I decided to make things easier and send her off at Neurostsos Inlet. She would probably end up somewhere in the Sound anyway, the same as if she was sent through the Narrows. 
I had bought her a new set of roses. They ended up being yellow roses because the pink ones at the store looked a little wilted. I also shortened the rudder. 

It was a dreary day in January when I loaded up the car with my kayak and drove down to Dawson’s Landing. Because I didn’t have anything like a wet suit, I had to wear my capri shorts and crocs (in January!)—so I was pretty cold! I timed the tides just right, and they were especially powerful that day! I paddled along in the kayak, north toward Quatsino Sound. When I looked back, my car was much further away than I thought it would be! I felt an urgency to get back, so I quickly let Chloe go. There was a lot of power in the water as she was taken away! I then turned around and paddled like the dickens, worrying that the strong current might carry me off too! When I got back to Dawson’s Landing, I decided to have a leisurely paddle along the shoreline in sheltered waters. It was interesting to be paddling the cold water on such a dark dreary day in the middle of winter! It was still relaxing and enjoyable. I was also somewhat relieved that my task had finally been done and that I could move on.

Occasionally, I wonder where Chloe’s sarcophagus is now. She might be tucked away in some cave along the Quatsino Sound, or she could very well have ended up on the open ocean, or she might even have made it to Japan! I wrote on her little box with an engraver, “Chloe, June 9th, 2009 to August 13th, 2020, RIP,” and attached a laminated business card for the blog on the front of her box. Maybe it will be discovered by archeologists someday! 

Chloe is one sweet soul who will be dearly missed.
